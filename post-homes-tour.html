The House That Taught Me How to Share

  • In Homes & Living
  • 3:00 PM, Dec 16
  • By Jordan Lee

The tour took fifteen minutes. Emma showed me my room—small, bright, enough space for a bed and desk. The shared kitchen. The parlor. Two bathrooms for five people. "That's it," she said. "Questions?" I had a thousand questions. I asked none of them. I moved in the next week.

I'd lived alone for eight years. My own apartment, my own schedule, my own everything. Privacy was normal. Sharing was what you did in college dorms, suffering through until you could afford your own place.

Then rent went up. Again. And I found myself at thirty-five, priced out of living alone, facing a choice: different city, or different living situation.

SILK Homes wasn't what I expected. The Ravenswood cottage is old—1892 old. Creaky floors, vintage fixtures, that particular smell of houses that have seen over a century of people living and leaving. But it's maintained, warm, full of afternoon light through wavy glass windows.

"We share common spaces," Emma explained during the tour. "Kitchen, living room, dining room. Bedrooms are private. We have a cleaning schedule, grocery system, house meetings once a month."

Living with people isn't about losing privacy. It's about gaining presence—someone to notice if you're okay, to share coffee with at odd hours, to remind you you're not alone. — Jordan Lee

The first month was adjustment. Learning whose footsteps were whose. Understanding the morning bathroom dance. Figuring out which shelf in the fridge was mine, which cookware was communal, when quiet hours started.

I expected friction. Rules broken, arguments over dishes, passive-aggressive notes about thermostats. College dorm drama at middle age.

Instead: Sarah making extra coffee because she heard me coming downstairs. Tom leaving a book on my door with a note: "thought you'd like this." Maya teaching me to cook actual vegetables instead of surviving on takeout. Bill fixing my bike without being asked.

Small kindnesses. Daily noticing. The opposite of isolation.

We're not best friends. We don't hang out constantly. Tom annoys me sometimes—he talks during movies, leaves beard trimmings in the sink. I probably annoy him back. But we coexist, and that coexistence has grown into something more: community.

Last month I got sick—actual flu, not just a cold. Stayed in bed for three days. Nobody lectured me about dishes or cleaning duties. Emma brought soup. Sarah checked on me. Maya left medicine outside my door with a note: "Take this. Sleep. We've got your chores."

I cried a little, alone in my room. When's the last time anyone took care of me? When's the last time anyone noticed I needed taking care of?

Living alone, you can disappear. Not die-and-nobody-finds-you dramatic—just fade. Skip meals. Stay up too late. Neglect yourself in small ways that accumulate. There's no witness, so there's no accountability.

Here, there's witness. Sarah notices if I skip breakfast. Tom asks if I'm okay when I'm quiet. Maya calls me out when I'm working too much. I do the same for them. We keep each other human.

The house teaches this. Victorian architecture designed for multi-generational living—parlors for gathering, kitchens built for cooking together, porches for sitting outside watching neighbors pass. Modern apartments are designed for isolation. These old houses remember community.

Would I go back to living alone? People ask this. I don't know. I miss some things—walking around naked, playing music loud at midnight, eating cereal for dinner without judgment. But I don't miss the loneliness. The coming home to empty rooms. The forgetting how to be around people.

This house taught me how to share. Not just kitchen space and bathroom schedules, but life. Presence. Attention. The knowledge that someone's listening when I come home, someone cares if I'm okay, someone will notice if I'm not.

Emma asked if I want to renew my room for another year. I said yes immediately. This Victorian cottage with its creaky floors and shared everything has become more home than any apartment I paid too much rent to live in alone.

The tour was fifteen minutes. Learning to live here is ongoing. But I'm learning. We all are. Together.
