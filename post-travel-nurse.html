Finding Home on the Road: A Travel Nurse's SILK Story

  • In Community & Homes
  • 5:30 PM, Dec 11
  • By Elena Martinez
  • 4,892 Views
  • 14 Comments
For three years, I lived out of a suitcase. Thirteen-week contracts, different cities, anonymous corporate housing. I was helping people heal while slowly coming apart myself. Elena Martinez, SILK Life

Travel nursing pays well. That's why most of us do it—student loans, saving for a house, catching up after divorce. The money is good. The lifestyle? Lonely in ways I didn't anticipate.

You arrive in a new city knowing no one. Corporate housing companies put you in sterile apartments—functional but soulless. Your coworkers are friendly but busy with their own lives. You work 12-hour shifts, sleep, and do it again. On days off, you explore alone. You send pictures to friends back home who've moved on without you. You tell yourself it's adventurous. Mostly, it's isolating.

Finding SILK Homes

My fifth contract was at a hospital near Parkersburg, West Virginia. I almost said no—I wanted Denver or Portland, somewhere "interesting." But the recruiter pushed, the pay was exceptional, and I was tired of arguing. Fine. West Virginia. Thirteen weeks. How bad could it be?

Instead of the usual corporate housing, I stumbled across SILK Homes online. "Intentional community for healthcare workers, travelers, and those seeking alternative living." It sounded hippie-ish. I'm not a hippie. But the photos showed actual houses with character, gardens, and the rent was better than furnished apartments. I emailed Sarah, who managed Ravenswood properties then.

"We're low-key," she wrote back. "Shared kitchen and living space, private bedrooms. Gardens if you want to use them. Community dinners sometimes but no pressure. People here value both solitude and connection. Might be a good fit for someone who's tired of anonymous living."

She got it. I signed a lease sight unseen.

I expected a place to sleep between shifts. What I found was a reason to stay awake. —  Elena Martinez

The First Month

I arrived exhausted from night shifts in Cleveland. The cottage at Ravenswood looked worn but loved—wide porch, original woodwork, a kitchen that felt lived-in rather than staged. My roommate Emma was a writer. We nodded hello, and she left me alone to unpack. Perfect.

Except the next morning, there was coffee made and a note: "Help yourself. Garden tomatoes on counter if you want." Such a small thing. In three years of travel nursing, no one had made coffee for me.

Advertisement

Over weeks, patterns emerged. Emma taught me to identify tomato varieties. An older neighbor named Bill showed me how to can salsa. Sunday evenings, people gathered on porches—no agenda, just sitting and talking as light faded over the river. I started joining.

My shifts were still brutal. Healthcare during staffing crises is traumatic. But coming home to a porch where someone might be reading, where I could sit quietly or share the day's difficulty—it softened things.

One Tuesday at 11:47 PM, I came home from the worst shift I'd had in months. We'd lost two patients in the ER—one expected, one suddenly and devastatingly not. I was hollowed out, that particular emptiness that comes from witnessing death up close.

Bill was on his porch, just sitting in the dark. He saw me walk up and didn't say anything. Just gestured to the empty chair. I sat. We didn't talk. After maybe twenty minutes, he said, "River's been here longer than any of us. It'll be here after." I nodded. That was enough.

Why It Matters

Travel nurses are modern nomads. We go where we're needed, work hard, and move on. The system assumes we're fine with transience. Most of us aren't.

What SILK offered wasn't just housing—it was context. A place where people knew my name and actually meant it when they asked how I was doing. Where I could mention a hard patient death and someone would just nod, understanding, rather than offering cheerful platitudes.

I learned to garden (badly). I adopted two chickens when Bill's flock had extras. I started looking forward to Sunday porch gatherings. When my 13-week contract ended, I extended. Then extended again. I've been here 18 months now, picking up local contracts rather than traveling.

"You could make more money in California," my recruiter reminds me. True. But I'd be alone in expensive housing, starting over socially every three months. Here, I have Emma's friendship, Bill's garden wisdom, Sunday evenings, and a sense of actual home.

For healthcare workers who serve others all day, SILK offers something rare: a place where we're also held, where our need for community matters as much as our ability to provide it. —  Elena Martinez

The trade-off is worth it. I make enough. I work with patients I see through recovery rather than handing them off mid-treatment. And I go home to a place that feels like more than a bed between shifts.

My pottery studio is in the converted sun porch now. I teach weekend classes to a rotating group of neighbors and whoever Emma invites over. Clay work is the opposite of ER nursing—slow, deliberate, patient. You can't rush a pot into being. It teaches me things I need to remember after 12-hour trauma shifts.

Last month, Terri from the hospital asked about SILK after I mentioned it during break. She's been doing travel contracts for two years, living the same isolated life I left behind. She moved into a room down the street three weeks ago. I made her coffee her first morning. Left tomatoes on the counter with a note.

The cycle continues. Community isn't something you find finished—it's something you keep building, one small gesture at a time. One coffee pot. One garden tomato. One quiet moment on a porch after the hardest night.

I came for thirteen weeks. I stayed for myself.
